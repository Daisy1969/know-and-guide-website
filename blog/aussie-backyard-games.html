Rebuilding Australian Backyard Games for iPhone and iPad

Updated: September 1, 2026   •   Category: iOS Game Development
Current status: actively in development.

Aussie Backyard Games is not yet an App Store release. The current build is being developed and play-tested as a collection of linked mini-games with persistent skills, unlockable items and a shared Backyard hub.

A lot of childhood games never had a board, a rulebook or even an agreed name. They were things kids invented because there was a swimming pool, a clothesline, a hose, a slippery patch of grass, a backyard and enough time to turn ordinary objects into a competition.

Aussie Backyard Games is an attempt to turn that memory into a playful iPhone and iPad game. The design is deliberately physical and slightly ridiculous. Rather than recreating polished organised sports, the mini-games are about momentum, timing, balance, strength, stamina, wind, water and the sort of rules that would have been negotiated five seconds before somebody yelled, “Go!”

The important part is the memory, not perfect simulation.

The game should feel like kids discovering what can be done with the backyard around them. The physics can be exaggerated, the scoring can be competitive and the characters can be silly, but each mini-game should still be recognisable as something that could have started with a group of kids and no equipment designed for the purpose.

The current backyard

🌊Whirlpool PoolRun around the pool to build a whirlpool, fight or use the current, push off the side, use floaties and weights, shove nearby players and try to stay in control as the water becomes increasingly chaotic.
👕Clothesline HoistUse strength and timing against a spinning clothesline, changing wind and flying clothes. Better pegs, clothing and persistent stamina change how long a player can hold on and how much chaos the line creates.
🧼Soap SlopeBuild speed, belly-flop before the line and slide as far as possible down a wet soapy slope. The camera follows the kid down the run while distance markers show whether the latest attempt beats the previous one.

A shared Backyard instead of separate apps

The mini-games live inside a central Backyard hub. Leaving a game returns the player to the Backyard, and progress carries between activities. The store is also shared so the player can see owned gear, persistent skills and upgrades rather than starting from zero every time a different backyard game is selected.

Persistent strength & staminaSkills matter across games and are intended to visibly change how long a kid can hold on, run or resist forces.
Unlockable gearFloaties, weights, clothes, pegs and other backyard objects can change a mini-game without turning it into a complicated inventory simulator.
Physical touch controlsTwo-finger running, holds, taps, swipes and contextual controls are designed around what the kid is actually doing in each game.
Readable mobile UIButtons, labels, score panels and store cards are being repeatedly adjusted so they remain readable on different iPhone and iPad screen sizes.
Kid-like physicsCharacters can be flung, dragged by water, slide awkwardly and recover. The animation does not need to look like professional sport.
Room to growThe Backyard structure makes it possible to add more remembered games later without rebuilding the whole app around each new idea.

Why the UI work matters as much as the physics

Much of the recent work has been less glamorous than adding a new mini-game: shrinking oversized store icons, increasing small item descriptions, improving text contrast, keeping Done and Backyard buttons away from the bottom edge, moving distance meters where the player can actually see them, and checking that controls stay on-screen across devices.

That iteration matters because these games are supposed to feel immediate. If a player has to hunt for the exit button or cannot read what an upgrade does, the simple backyard idea gets buried under interface friction.

Balancing skills so upgrades actually feel different

Strength and stamina are intended to change more than a number in a store. A higher stamina level should create a visibly larger or more capable stamina reserve; greater strength should slow stamina loss in strength-heavy games; AI kids need their own working stamina logic instead of being thrown from a clothesline while their bar appears frozen.

Those systems are still being tuned. The aim is for progression to make the player feel more capable while leaving enough unpredictability that a backyard game can still produce a ridiculous loss.

What comes next

The immediate work is continued polishing of the existing mini-games, controls, camera behaviour, physics and shared UI. After that, the Backyard can expand with more remembered games and variations.

This is one of the projects where the development process is part of the point: take a very personal memory, reduce it to the few mechanics that made it fun, then use iPhone and iPad hardware to make it playable again without making it too clean or too serious.
