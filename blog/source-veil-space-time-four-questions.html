Four Questions About the Source, the Veil, Space and Time

An evolving idea by Michael Harvey, explored through questions, evidence and the limits of what we know.

5 November 2026 · Know & Guide · Exploratory discussion

Updated 13 November 2026: could celestial holography be the missing link?

Updated 6 November 2026: the plate, the shadow and moving antimatter.

Status: This is an evidence-led exploration of Michael Harvey's developing hypothesis. We can begin investigating now, without a completed theorem: look for relevant observations, compare explanations, and follow findings that support or challenge the idea. The full source-and-veil hypothesis has not been experimentally confirmed.

The idea we are trying to understand

Michael's starting question is whether the world we experience could arise through a transformation: a source containing the potential for space and time, a patterned veil, and the emergence of the space and time familiar to us.

The source has not yet been defined as ordinary light, energy or information. “Transformation” names the question we want to investigate; it does not supply a known physical process.

The relationship between the veil and the “chain” remains open. The chain might be part of the veil, or they might be two descriptions of the same thing. Michael also asks whether black holes could play a return role, taking something back towards the source. What that “something” would be, and what a return would mean physically, have not yet been defined.

Those uncertainties give us questions to investigate. Michael's aim is to seek evidence and see whether the idea works, rather than to announce a new theorem. We can start with four questions, asking what is already known, what that knowledge supports, and where another explanation fits just as well or better.

1. Can light passing through a patterned veil help us understand the idea?

Short answer: It provides a useful analogy for how a pattern can shape what appears. It does not yet provide a mechanism for producing matter or spacetime.

Optical holography offers a concrete starting point. An interference pattern can record information about a light wavefront. With suitable illumination, a hologram reconstructs a wavefront that gives an image with depth. Dennis Gabor's Nobel lecture explains the wave behaviour behind this process. A sheet can therefore carry information used to reconstruct a three-dimensional-looking scene. It has not created the material object represented in that scene. [1]

For Michael's proposal, the helpful question is what corresponds to each element: the source, the pattern, the transformation and the resulting experience. “Light through a veil” currently describes a way of thinking about these relationships. It does not establish that the proposed source is ordinary light, that the veil is a physical sheet, or that the universe is an optical hologram.

There is also an important distinction between optical holography and holographic ideas in theoretical physics. Similar language can suggest an analogy, but a shared word is not a shared mechanism.

Evidence worth following: Light can also participate in the production of particles through a different physical process. In SLAC's E144 experiment, a high-energy electron beam boosted laser photons to much higher energies. Interactions between those photons and additional laser photons produced a positron signal consistent with electron–positron pair creation. This is experimental evidence for a specific particle-production process, explained by quantum electrodynamics. It takes place within existing spacetime, unlike the proposed emergence of spacetime itself. A hologram reconstructing an image and photons participating in pair creation should therefore be examined separately. [10]

This gives us a concrete comparison to explore: which part of Michael's intuition concerns the formation of appearances, which concerns particles, and which concerns space and time? Evidence for one does not automatically establish the others, but each can help sharpen the investigation.

What we need to clarify: What does the veil do? Does it select possibilities, encode relationships, constrain transformations, or something else? If the chain and veil are the same, what is gained by using both names? If they differ, how do they interact?

2. Can relationships give rise to space?

Short answer: There are serious research approaches in which geometric relationships emerge from more basic quantum structure. Their results do not establish Michael's proposed source or veil.

Evidence worth following: The Ryu–Takayanagi relation connects entanglement entropy, a quantity describing quantum correlations, with the area of a particular surface in a corresponding gravitational geometry. It is a precise mathematical relation in specified holographic models using the AdS/CFT correspondence. This provides a concrete reason to investigate connections between quantum information and geometry, while leaving open whether those models describe the origin of our own universe. [11]

Mark Van Raamsdonk's work explores a connection between quantum entanglement and connected spacetime in a particular quantum-gravity setting. In the examples considered, changing entanglement changes how regions are geometrically connected. This gives a precise research context for asking whether space is fundamental or emerges from underlying relationships. It is not an observation showing that every atom in our universe is directly entangled with every other atom. [2]

Alex Wissner-Gross's recently announced Chain Theory takes a different, explicit starting point: a proposed network built from finite-dimensional quantum systems and entangled pairs. Its first paper presents a candidate value for the fine-structure constant, with the physical identification of the calculated quantity still conjectural. Numerical agreement and mathematical checks do not, by themselves, verify the proposed account of nature. [3], [4]

Michael's addition needs to remain distinct. Chain Theory begins with the network as its basic account of what exists. Michael asks about a source containing the potential for the experienced world, and a veil or chain through which a transformation takes place. That is an additional proposal, rather than a conclusion already supplied by Chain Theory.

What we need to clarify: How would the proposed relationships give rise to distance, neighbourhood and dimension? Can those quantities be calculated from a defined structure, rather than drawn onto it afterwards? What would the source contribute that the network alone does not?

3. Can time emerge too?

Short answer: Quantum research provides specific ways to describe time through correlations. Applying that idea to the whole universe, or to Michael's proposed source, requires further work.

An experiment by Ekaterina Moreva and colleagues illustrates the Page–Wootters mechanism using two entangled photons. One subsystem serves as a clock for the other. Correlations can describe change for an internal observer even when the combined state is described as static. This is an experimental illustration in a controlled system, not proof that all cosmic time is generated in this way. [5]

Michael's question is whether space and time might exist as potential at the source and become the space and time we experience through transformation. That raises a useful difficulty: words such as “before,” “becomes” and “returns” already suggest time. If ordinary time is supposed to emerge, the model must explain what orders these processes without quietly assuming the very thing it is trying to explain.

It may be possible to distinguish an underlying relation or order from the time measured by clocks. That is a question for the model to resolve, not an answer we can insert on Michael's behalf.

What we need to clarify: Does “potential for time” mean possible sequences, correlations that can serve as clocks, or something else? What determines the direction of change? How would the resulting description connect with the time measured in physical experiments?

4. How could we investigate whether the idea works?

Short answer: Start with one part of the hypothesis, examine the evidence for it, and compare the alternatives. A completed theorem is not a prerequisite for beginning that inquiry.

A practical path would be:

  1. Choose one claim. For example, does the question concern patterns producing an image, relationships defining geometry, correlations serving as clocks, or a possible black-hole return? Keep the meaning of that claim clear enough to investigate.
  2. Examine the relevant evidence. Separate a laboratory observation, a mathematical result and an analogy. Record exactly which part of the claim each supports, and which parts it leaves open.
  3. Compare competing explanations. Ask whether established physics already explains the observation. A result compatible with several explanations cannot, by itself, tell us which one is right.
  4. Look for constraints and challenges. Seek null results, contrary observations and conditions under which the idea would fail. These can narrow the possibilities and improve the hypothesis.
  5. Build a small model where useful. Represent one relationship or process, state its assumptions, and see whether it reproduces something already measured. A simple illustration may clarify the idea without proving it describes nature.
  6. Seek a distinguishing prediction when possible. If the model suggests a difference from other explanations, derive its size and conditions before collecting a new result. Then design suitable controls, account for uncertainty and invite independent replication.

The Holometer offers a useful example of the difference between a broad idea and a defined test. Its first-generation measurements constrained specified models of correlated geometrical shear noise using two interferometers. Those limits apply to the models and signals the instrument could test. They do not settle every possible holographic proposal, and they are not a test of Michael's present idea. [6]

We can begin the first four steps with the evidence discussed here. A hologram demonstration can clarify the optical analogy; it would not test the cosmological hypothesis. A genuinely distinguishing experiment will need a derived prediction, but that comes after useful investigation has already begun. No specific signal or measurement threshold has yet been derived for the source-and-veil proposal.

Where do black holes and antimatter fit?

Question: Does the Moonshot discussion connect with this idea?

Answer: It raises related subjects, but it does not establish the proposed connection. In the episode Michael shared, the discussion from around 1:21:06 concerns trapping and studying antimatter, including a positron, the electron's antiparticle. The speakers discuss precision tests of physics and speculate about propulsion. At around 1:26:24, a brief aside imagines feeding a microscopic black hole with waste to obtain energy. These are different topics with different evidence requirements. [7]

The Northwestern work behind the antimatter discussion is real experimental progress: researchers report holding a single positron for months and controlling its quantum state in a quantum cyclotron. This prepares the way for a future precision comparison of the positron's and electron's magnetic moments, relevant to testing CPT symmetry. It is not an established new magnetic-moment result, and it should not be described as a general record for storing antimatter. [8], [9]

The engineering possibilities discussed in a podcast should not be presented as demonstrated technologies. Nor does the black-hole aside provide evidence for a return to an underlying source. It is a useful prompt to sharpen Michael's question: what, specifically, would his proposed return process do that existing physics does not already describe?

Michael's suggestion that black holes may return something to the source is an open part of the proposal. A useful treatment must distinguish the established physics of black holes from this interpretation, and specify what is proposed to return, by what process, and with what observable consequences. The mention of antimatter in a discussion does not itself provide that missing mechanism.

Evidence that any extension must accommodate: An analysis of the gravitational-wave event GW150914 found support for the black-hole area law: the final horizon area was consistent with being at least as large as the combined initial areas. That inference uses measured gravitational waves and modelling assumptions. It is a concrete observation for any proposal that changes the behaviour of black-hole mergers to address. It does not reveal an underlying destination for matter or information, and cannot establish a return to Michael's proposed source. [12]

Keeping the question open without losing rigour

There are already things to investigate. Holography demonstrates wavefront reconstruction. E144 provides evidence for a particular photon-mediated particle-production process. Holographic research connects quantum correlations and geometry in defined models. Quantum-clock experiments illustrate relational time, while black-hole observations and the Holometer put constraints on particular physical claims.

These findings give us starting points and checks, rather than a verdict on the whole hypothesis. Michael's source, veil and possible return through black holes remain open questions. We can follow the evidence one claim at a time, welcome results that strengthen or weaken the idea, and revise it when another explanation fits better. The invitation is to investigate and find out.

Development note: the plate, the shadow and moving antimatter

6 November 2026 · A record of the discussion as it develops

Michael asked to record this stage of the idea while keeping its uncertainty clear. He is exploring possibilities and trying to bring them together, without claiming that the hypothesis is correct. The original four questions above remain as the starting point.

A coloured-glass plate and a separate obstruction

The analogy is now colours and objects held between two pieces of glass. Source passes through this patterned plate or veil, producing what, in the proposal, becomes the experienced universe. A separate speck of dirt on the plate interrupts that passage. Michael suggests that the resulting shadow-like effect could be what appears within the universe as antimatter. In this clarified version, the plate itself is neither the speck nor something assumed to be made of antimatter.

He pictures multiple specks, with their effects perceptible against the surrounding source-lit projection. This records the proposed analogy; it does not establish how much antimatter exists or how scientists detect it.

A possible return cycle, with residue

Michael also considers whether source could be finite. He connects this with his earlier question about black holes gathering the projected “leftovers” and returning something to source. Perhaps some returning material lands on the plate instead of fully rejoining source, leaving the specks that interrupt the projection. Both the return and the residue are tentative additions. There is no established evidence that black holes return material to such a source or deposit it on a veil.

Could a fixed speck have a moving effect?

His current suggestion is that a speck might remain fixed after landing, like the rest of the plate's pattern, while changes in source or the projection make its effect move within the experienced universe. He remains unsure about the mechanism and whether specks eventually clear. In ordinary optics, a fixed obstruction can cast a moving shadow when the illumination or projection geometry changes. That offers an analogy to examine; it is not evidence that a hidden plate underlies physical particles.

What the antimatter evidence tells us

Antimatter has measurable physical properties. A positron has the electron's mass and the opposite electric charge; it can interact with matter and annihilate with an electron. An ordinary optical shadow has none of those particle properties. Scientists detect antimatter through physical interactions, not merely because light or matter surrounds a gap. Connecting the proposed interruption to actual antiparticles therefore remains an unanswered part of the idea. [13]

  • Motion can be changed: the ALPHA collaboration demonstrated laser cooling of magnetically trapped antihydrogen in 2021. Laser light reduced the anti-atoms' kinetic energy, providing experimental evidence that their motion can be controlled. [14]
  • Gravity affects that motion: ALPHA's 2023 experiment released trapped antihydrogen and measured where it annihilated in the apparatus. The results showed downward motion consistent, within the experiment's precision, with Earth's attractive gravity. The measurement has uncertainty and leaves room for more precise comparisons of how matter and antimatter fall. [15]

Motion is described relative to a physical reference frame. An antiparticle may be moving relative to the laboratory while momentarily at rest relative to something moving alongside it. This does not make its movement merely subjective: instruments record interactions at particular places and times. These experiments establish measurable antimatter behaviour, without establishing Michael's proposed source, plate or shadow mechanism.

Questions to carry forward: What changes in the proposed projection when an antiparticle moves? How could an interruption account for mass, charge, annihilation and observed pair production? If source is finite, what quantity is conserved through projection, return and residue? We can compare these possibilities with existing evidence now, while leaving the answers open and recording what supports or challenges each step.

13 November development note: Could celestial holography be the missing link?

In Sabrina Pasterski’s New Scientist interview, celestial holography raises a question that gets close to the Chain idea: could relationships on a two-dimensional surface describe what happens in a world with three dimensions of space and one of time?

There is a concrete point of contact. In a paper by Pasterski, Shu-Heng Shao and Andrew Strominger, a particular scattering calculation in four-dimensional flat spacetime is recast as a correlation on a two-dimensional sphere of directions. The two dimensions are angles on a mathematical boundary at infinity. Pasterski’s review of celestial holography explains how the wider programme reorganises scattering data and what remains open.

There is also a more specific link to the idea of connection. In a preprint on the connected wedge theorem, Athira Arayath and Pasterski relate the ability of signals to meet in certain spacetime setups to connectedness in the holographic description. This is a result about particular regions and conditions. It does not establish a direct link between every pair of things.

For Chain, the possible missing link is a rule that translates patterns of relationships into geometry. Could the plate represent a network of information, with an apparent depth recovered from its relationships? The papers give examples of how two descriptions can correspond. They do not establish a physical plate, an external source flowing through it, or a boundary that physically produces our universe.

First test: Build a toy network with only two spatial coordinates and a rule for pulses to travel between nodes. Generate pulse responses and set some aside before fitting either model. Ask whether a model with an inferred depth predicts the withheld arrival times better than a two-dimensional model, using comparably flexible models and no preassigned depth or 3D distances. Check that the result holds for repeated simulations and does not also appear when the links are scrambled. Even a positive result would establish a property of this toy model. A physical theory would still need to reproduce a known scattering result and make a distinctive prediction that could fail.
